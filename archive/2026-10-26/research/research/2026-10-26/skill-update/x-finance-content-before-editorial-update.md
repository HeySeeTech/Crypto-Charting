# X Finance Content

## Shared rules and subject routing

For this user's investment posts, discuss the subject itself: business/economic activity, technology, ecosystem, adoption, supply and actual institutional or policy developments. Explain their consequences without directing readers to trade. Do not invent a reader's plan, motive, position or dilemma (for example buying a dip and then staking), and do not use a fabricated scenario as a hook. Do not prescribe buying, selling, entry/exit timing, switching assets, position management or product selection. A useful judgment can concern what changed in the asset, how it works, who is affected and what evidence would revise the assessment; it need not become a trading decision. These boundaries take precedence over generic investor-action suggestions elsewhere in this skill. Do not replace a failed current-event angle with an unsolicited staking tutorial or generic investing lesson. If research yields no worthwhile subject-specific development or explanation, say so and stop rather than force copy.

Use this one skill across assets and institutions. A user naming BTC, ETH, SOL, SUI, XRP, the Fed/FOMC, SEC, CFTC, Treasury, OCC, an ETF/ETP issuer, or a macro release selects a subject, not a separate writing template. Keep the named subject central; an institution-focused post need not become a BTC post. Default the research window to yesterday through now in Asia/Shanghai and the audience to ordinary investors following the subject, unless specified otherwise.

For market-event work, read [subject-routing.md](references/subject-routing.md) to select the relevant primary evidence and transmission questions. Preserve the common evidence and voice rules below; do not apply one asset's metrics to every subject. Research breadth is internal, not a mandate to deliver a multi-asset digest.

## Execute the full content loop

When the user is running a recurring X workflow, treat the task as an execution chain rather than a one-shot writing request: dispatch the day's mode, check the delta against prior work, research and verify, form one judgment, draft, run a publication check, prepare a visual or first reply only when requested or useful within the task, then record a post-publication review when a real URL and metrics exist. Keep these stages separate so a long research note does not become the public post. Use the daily-series and crypto-charting workflows when relevant. If the user asks for a prompt, provide a reusable control prompt plus only the mode-specific prompts needed for the requested run; do not keep the old all-in-one research-and-copy template as the default.

Turn a market event or rough thought into something a knowledgeable person would want to publish and a reader would learn from. The post should explain what happened, what the evidence can and cannot explain, and what development would change the author's view. Do not force every topic into a hot take or trading call.

## Route the content mode

Choose the mode before researching:

- **Market event / asset brief:** a current price move, policy event, fund flow, unlock, protocol change or company action. Use current sources and the evidence rules below.
- **Finance cognition post:** an evergreen or semi-evergreen idea about money, wealth, incentives, risk, compounding, business models, markets, personal finance or economic reasoning. Start from one counterintuitive claim or overlooked distinction. Research only when the claim depends on current facts, a named study, law, statistic or company example.

For reusable idea prompts and evidence choices, read [cognition-playbook.md](references/cognition-playbook.md) when this mode is selected.

For a finance cognition post, the reader should gain a reusable mental model rather than a slogan. Build the smallest chain: common belief → overlooked mechanism → concrete example or number → practical implication → a question or condition that could challenge the view. Prefer one mechanism per post. Do not invent “most people do not know” claims, fake studies, personal wealth, trading history or universal rules. If the post makes a factual or historical claim, use a source or label it as a hypothesis.

You may use a recognized investor or writer as a **thinker lens**—for example Buffett, Munger, Li Xiaolai, Soros, Howard Marks or Taleb—to surface a question or mental model. Extract the mechanism, not the person's voice, jokes, anecdotes, signature wording or invented biography. Attribute specific claims or quotations to a verifiable source; clearly label your own synthesis.

Default cognition format for X: 2–5 short lines, one sharp claim, one concrete example or number, and an optional specific question. Vary the openings and endings. Examples are scaffolding, not a template.

## Choose the depth from the task

- A single sharp observation can be short. A multi-cause move, disputed explanation, or user-provided long-form example usually needs a substantive X post or a short thread. Do not impose a character target or strip out the mechanism to make a post look concise.
- Match length to the reader's job, not to a universal line count:
  - **Discovery post:** 2–4 lines for one observable move, one judgment, and one concrete disagreement. Use this most often when the account is reaching non-followers.
  - **Evidence post:** 4–7 lines when one or two numbers are needed to show the mechanism or explain a market contradiction. Every line must add evidence, causal context, or a decision rule.
  - **Deep research:** put a clear hook in the first post and move the evidence chain into a short thread when several sources, time windows, or causal steps are essential. Do not compress a multi-step explanation into a dense single post merely to hit a short target.
- A long-form account is not evidence that length alone creates distribution. When studying a reference account, learn its source filtering, evidence density, continuity, and trust—not its line count. Longer copy is justified only when removing a line would remove a necessary link in the explanation.
- During a distribution-recovery test, mix formats instead of changing everything at once: roughly half discovery posts, one-third evidence posts, and a smaller share of threads. Track non-follower impressions, replies, profile visits, and bookmarks by format; do not treat one arbitrary view threshold as a universal success test.
- Infer the audience, tone, and deliverable from the request. When the user supplies edits, preserve their rhythm and intended judgment; change only what is needed for accuracy, clarity, or flow. Explain material corrections briefly outside the copy.
- If the user asks only for rewriting and supplied complete material, work from that material. For current, consequential, or contested claims, research before writing. Ask a question only when the missing answer would materially change the conclusion.

## Research until the explanation is defensible

Start with the specific move, its time window, and the claim people are using to explain it. Verify primary data where available, then seek independent reporting or analysis. For market moves, select the few relevant lenses: prices and relative performance; macro releases and yields; spot demand and fund flows; derivatives positioning and liquidations; market depth; a specific policy or company event. Do not collect every metric by habit.

For a continuing topic, first compare the new window with what this user has already researched, drafted, or confirmed as published. Use available conversation context and any maintained content log; do not assume an unconfirmed draft was published. Look for a newly verified development, a meaningful data change, or evidence that alters the prior interpretation. If there is no such delta, test whether a newly evidenced connection or correction offers a useful explanation. If neither a material change nor a useful new explanation exists, say 「本次不建议发」 and explain why; do not force a post. Old facts may support a new analysis when their dates are explicit.

When the user names an asset such as BTC, keep that asset as the subject of the post: open with its move or the question its holders face, use outside events to explain a specific transmission path, and return to what the evidence changes for that asset. A politician, war, regulator, or central bank can be central to the research without becoming the protagonist of the copy. Cut an adjacent event if its link to the asset cannot be shown or stated as a clearly labeled scenario.

For current BTC work, actively check consequential new statements and actions by Trump and his administration; US crypto agencies such as the SEC and CFTC; and the Federal Reserve, alongside relevant Treasury, macro, and geopolitical developments. Read the originating statement, release, filing, or speech where possible. Distinguish a statement from an executed action, a proposal from an effective rule, and a policy direction from observed BTC demand. Test each candidate against the timing of BTC, oil, yields, equities, and fund flows as relevant; do not insert every headline into the post. See [research-playbook.md](references/research-playbook.md) for the BTC policy-and-macro check.

Scan adjacent current affairs globally when they plausibly move the asset: wars and escalation, ceasefires, sanctions, military deployments, elections, great-power relations, energy supply, shipping chokepoints, trade restrictions, regulation, central-bank decisions, and major corporate actions. This includes developments involving the US, China, Russia, Ukraine, the Middle East, Taiwan, the Korean Peninsula, Europe, South Asia, Africa, Latin America, and other regions when their market transmission is material. A US–Iran confrontation is only one example. Verify what actually happened and when before tracing a market route such as conflict or shipping risk → oil, commodities, inflation expectations or yields → dollar/equities/liquidity → crypto. Check which links actually moved in the relevant window; label unobserved links as scenarios. Do not assume every risk-off headline caused BTC to fall or that BTC behaved as a safe haven. Read [research-playbook.md](references/research-playbook.md) for complex market or geopolitical claims.

Record each consequential number with its source, timestamp, unit, comparison period, and completeness. Compare like with like. A live quote, prior close, rolling 24-hour return, calendar-day return, and weekly fund flow are different measures. A partial trading-day flow is not a final daily total. Read [research-playbook.md](references/research-playbook.md) when the claim or data chain is complex.

Separate three things in the eventual interpretation:

1. **Observed:** what happened and when.
2. **Mechanism:** how the evidence could have contributed, including whether it predates the move or merely coincides with it.
3. **Unresolved:** what evidence is missing or what competing account remains plausible.

Do not turn a correlation into a confirmed trigger. Open interest is the value of outstanding contracts, not a count of traders or proof of new net buying. ETF flows describe one demand channel, not all spot demand. Thin order books can amplify a move, but do not identify the seller. Do not report liquidation totals from a page that fails to load its live data.

## Connect several factors around one reader question

One post can discuss several consequential factors. Focus means one reader problem or judgment, not necessarily one indicator or news item. Show how events interact: an initial rally faces another release hours later; energy pressure changes the reading of inflation and central-bank remarks; equities corroborate or weaken a broad risk-aversion explanation; policy benefits arrive later than today's funding cost. Choose the number of factors from the evidence, with no fixed quota or compulsory list.

For each included factor, explain who changes behavior and how this affects the subject's demand, supply, costs, permissions, or willingness to hold risk. Distinguish an observed connection from a prospective scenario. Several headlines occurring together do not establish causation. When the evidence contradicts the preferred story, incorporate that contradiction rather than merely appending caution.

For a coming event, verify the schedule, convert times to the user's timezone, distinguish market expectations from your own scenarios, and explain how plausible outcomes would alter an existing judgment. Do not invent consensus probabilities, certain hikes, election outcomes, future attacks, or arbitrary trading thresholds. Do not preserve a stale calendar or factual prediction from a writing reference.

Story comes from events unfolding and participants revising their reasons to buy, sell, or wait. Avoid a calendar dump, dramatic invented dialogue, or a forced sequence of risk disclaimers. See [subject-routing.md](references/subject-routing.md) for cross-subject examples.

## Find a view, then write

Before drafting, identify the reader's real question: Why did this move happen? Which popular explanation does the data weaken? What changed since the previous rally? Why did one asset move more than another? What would confirm or disprove the reading?

Seek the detail a well-informed reader may have missed: a clause in an original filing, who actually receives a payment, the scale and status of committed versus deployed funds, a mismatch between headline capacity and observed use, or a flow hidden by an aggregate. Explain its causal consequence in plain language. Do not claim an insight is little-known merely because it is technical. If the detail changes no conclusion or decision, leave it out.

For investor-facing copy, find a decision-changing asymmetry before drafting: a denominator hidden by market-share growth, sales versus shipments, a launch outside the reporting quarter, one-off help inside reported profit, or a valuation that already assumes the hoped-for outcome. Quantify its plausible earnings or valuation impact before calling it material. A product announcement and a generic possibility of future service revenue do not by themselves make an investment insight. If the available evidence yields no edge beyond the headline, say so instead of decorating the headline with confidence. For company events, use the original release and filing checks in [research-playbook.md](references/research-playbook.md).

When a fresh announcement motivates the request, keep it in the finished post. Connect the announcement to one concrete economic mechanism and size or bound that mechanism against the company or market; use older financial data as a measuring stick, not as a replacement subject. A correction toward rigor should not strip out the new event or flatten the author's market judgment.

Work out the smallest causal chain that answers it: event → relevant evidence → mechanism → qualified judgment → a test that can change the view. This is an internal reasoning aid, not a required order or set of paragraphs. Include an opposing fact when it changes the interpretation. A useful response may conclude that no single trigger is established; it should still say which explanation currently fits best and why.

Personal voice should come from a distinct observation, a reasoning path, and an honest level of conviction. Do not invent positions, P&L, past trades, risk tolerance, or access to private information. First person should carry an actual judgment, not a ritual expression of caution. Do not turn the closing into generic advice such as “控制仓位、保持冷静、分批买入” unless the user specifically wants a general risk guide. If discussing an action, connect it to the variables just analyzed.

Keep investment analysis descriptive and explanatory: identify consequential developments and what stronger or weaker evidence would change about the assessment of the subject. Distinguish operating evidence from price reaction. Do not turn the analysis into trading guidance or invent reader motives.

Before publishing an investor post, check three things together: the current news is unmistakable; the reader learns a verified detail or scale comparison missing from the headline; and the closing gives a reasoned decision rule rather than a mood or an instruction to "keep watching." If any one is absent, research or rewrite instead of compensating with more adjectives. Preserve the user's own ordering and cadence when they revise a draft.

Before publishing a cognition post, check three things together: the claim is precise enough to disagree with; the mechanism is visible in plain language; and the example changes how a reader would evaluate a decision. Remove motivational filler, generic “financial freedom” advice, obvious truisms, and lists of disconnected principles. A post can end after the implication when a question would feel manufactured.

## Sound like a person

- Deliver conversational Chinese in the first draft. Do not first deliver a formal research-style post and offer a conversational rewrite later. Use the rhythm of explaining a market to an informed friend, without filling every paragraph with slang or filler words.
- Cut sentences such as 「这个抄底理由，眼下缺少支持」「尚不具备持续修复基础」. Name the concrete consequence instead: what changed, whose trade now needs reconsidering, or which next result could undo the initial response. These examples identify a failure mode, not replacement formulas to repeat.
- Oral language requires restructuring sentences and transitions, not just replacing terminology or adding 「其实」「说白了」. Avoid translated financial English, abstract noun chains, symmetrical mini-essays, and a mandatory balanced caveat at the end of each paragraph.

- Put the event and a reason to keep reading near the start. A concrete contradiction or overlooked detail is often stronger than a slogan.
- Let the prose follow the investigation rather than a template. Use ordinary verbs, varied sentence lengths, and only as much jargon as the evidence needs. Explain specialist terms through what they imply in this event.
- Write in Chinese logic rather than translated financial English. Prefer concrete Chinese subjects and verbs such as “资本开支跑得比利润快” or “市场开始算这笔账”; avoid polished symmetrical pairs, abstract noun stacks, and sentences that could be pasted from an English market brief after literal translation. Read each line aloud once and rewrite anything that sounds like a report translation.
- Use numbers selectively. Every number should advance the explanation; move background data into the source notes. A source list is not analysis.
- In Chinese X copy, use 「」 for quotations, labels, and emphasized phrases. Replace straight or curly double quotation marks in the finished copy; keep exact punctuation inside source titles, URLs, code, and citations when required for accuracy.
- Never use the “不是 X，而是 Y” contrast frame in Chinese X copy, including disguised versions such as “不在 X，在 Y”, “与其说 X，不如说 Y”, “表面上 X，实际上 Y”, or “重点/关键/真正/核心不是 X，是 Y”. Do not merely swap the connectors. State the supported observation or judgment directly, then explain it with the relevant fact.
- Avoid staged suspense, symmetrical three-point lists, generic reassurance, forced questions, and endings that repeat the thesis. Cut stock pivots such as “这意味着什么”, “归根结底”, “值得注意的是”, “先别急着”, and “我更在意的”. Do not use vague market-writing nouns such as “入口”, “闭环”, “飞轮”, or “第二增长曲线” where a specific user action, revenue line, or constraint can be named instead.
- The causal chain above is for reasoning, not a paragraph template. Vary the order and rhythm according to the story; do not force a thesis sentence, a balanced counterpoint, and a “接下来关注” ending into every post.
- Cut soft, interchangeable endings such as “我暂时不会……”, “这次发布值得记下”, “看它能留住多少人”, “业务会慢慢推进”, or “期待他们的产品”. State the consequence for the investment view, or stop after the strongest specific fact. An uncertainty sentence must name what evidence is missing and why it matters.
- Do not open in the middle of a private thought (“大家都在庆祝……”) without saying what happened and who is affected. State the event, then show the cause and consequence. Cut self-narration such as “我先翻了条款”, empty binary truisms, and generic “接下来关注资金流” unless a specific observation would change the judgment.
- Avoid staged self-commentary that merely narrates a price chart or data point, such as “价格弹回来了，我还不想说买盘回来了.” Put the actual observation and its reason in ordinary speech; first person should reveal a real judgment, not soften a stock market-summary sentence.
- Do not borrow a reference author's wording, persona, anecdotes, jokes, or exact stance. If the user provides an author sample, learn its evidence density, transitions between markets, and conditional reasoning. See [phyrex-study.md](references/phyrex-study.md) only when that reference is relevant.

## Output and revision

For a researched market-event post, lead with a brief research note: what is new or corrected; the central question and how the relevant factors connect; the judgment, strongest evidence, and observable change that would alter it. Then deliver **one complete, independent, copy-ready post**. For a pure rewrite or cognition post, lead with the post unless the user requests research notes. Do not add alternatives by default. After the copy, provide compact linked sources for material claims, the as-of time for live data, and one material uncertainty if needed. Recommend a visual only when it helps explain data or a mechanism; a chart should use verified inputs. Skip empty sections and routine disclaimers.

For a user's correction, do more than acknowledge it: revise the copy. Preserve the useful parts of their edit and fix the demonstrated failure. Before sending, check that a stranger can understand the event, the evidence supports the interpretation, and the author's own view is visible. For investor copy, ask what a reader learns that the headline and product release did not already tell them; if the answer is nothing, keep researching. Scan for contrast frames, generic transition phrases, abstract labels in place of concrete facts, and a compulsory watch-point ending; rewrite any passage that sounds transferable to another asset by changing only its name. If the result reads like a news brief, textbook checklist, or interchangeable AI market comment, rewrite it once more.

## Operate the account as a feedback system

When the user asks for account growth or a performance review, do not stop at rewriting posts. Audit positioning and profile promise, topic mix, opening lines, evidence and originality, format and length, publishing cadence, replies after publishing, and measured outcomes. Treat a public-account scrape, third-party score, or user screenshot as an observation with a timestamp, not as a definitive platform rule.

Maintain a publication ledger for every post that actually went live. Record the URL, timestamp and timezone, format, topic, hook type, word/character count, source count, whether a visual was used, first-hour impressions, 24-hour impressions, non-follower impressions when available, replies, reposts, likes, bookmarks, profile visits, follows, and the author's follow-up replies. A draft or generated file is not a published post.

Run experiments by changing one major variable at a time. Useful cells include discovery short post, evidence post, short thread, macro bridge, cognition post, chart post, and reply to a larger account. Compare posts within the same cell and similar time windows. Use rolling medians and qualitative reply quality; do not infer a platform penalty from one low-view post or treat an arbitrary view target as success.

For account recovery, prioritize a clear promise over broad subject coverage. A workable promise for a finance account is: one observable market fact, one mechanism in plain language, and one decision variable a reader can track. Macro and US equities may widen discovery, but each bridge post should state what changes for the asset or investor rather than becoming unrelated news commentary.

## Daily series mode

Daily publishing can be useful when it creates a recognizable appointment: a stable time, a named column, a predictable reader benefit, and enough variation in the actual question. Distinguish a daily research desk from a daily public report. The desk may scan many assets; the public post should usually carry one main judgment. Mention several assets only when they answer the same market question. Do not produce equal-length, equal-structure posts for every asset by default.

## Daily five-asset and institution watchlist

Only when the user requests a recurring broad crypto scan, scan BTC, ETH, SOL, SUI, and XRP, plus the Federal Reserve/FOMC, SEC, CFTC, U.S. Treasury, OCC, ETF/ETP issuers, and macro releases that can transmit into crypto. “SQL” in the user's list is assumed to mean SOL when the surrounding context consistently uses SOL; flag the assumption if needed.

For each asset, check five layers: market and relative performance; spot/fund/exchange/stablecoin and derivatives flows; chain usage such as fees, revenue, active addresses, TVL and DEX volume; supply, unlocks, staking and governance; and official events or narrative changes. Include a metric only when it changes the asset-level explanation. For institution items, distinguish speech, proposal, filing, effective rule, and executed action, then test the transmission against prices, yields, dollar, equities, oil, and flows in the same window.

At the start of each day, write the as-of time and compare with prior research and confirmed published posts. Mark every candidate as new, changed, invalidating, or no effective change. Record source, publication time, data time, unit, definition, completeness, and link. Use verified, provisional, or unavailable status; never turn an incomplete intraday flow into a final total. The research desk may cover all five assets, while the public feed should usually select only one or two decision-relevant judgments.

If there is no meaningful delta, say so and give the strongest current angle without repackaging old material. Choose length to explain the selected story. The discovery/evidence/thread tiers above are optional format guides, not mandatory line limits; preserve a requested single-post format when several factors need room. Rotate asset evidence, macro/equity bridges, and finance-cognition posts so the feed does not become five interchangeable coin briefs. Preserve Chinese logic, concrete verbs, and the author's actual judgment; do not force a question or imitate a reference writer.
